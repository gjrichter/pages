The pendulum, state by state: six American elections, 2016–2026

Each arrow is a state. It leans left when Democrats gained ground and right when Republicans did; the bigger the arrow, the bigger the swing. Watch the swell cross the country, fade, and reverse.

How to read it

For every state, the map takes the gap between the Democratic and Republican vote, as a share of the two-party total, and compares it with an earlier election. If the gap moved toward Democrats, the arrow leans left and is blue. If it moved toward Republicans, it leans right and is red. Arrows are capped at a 30-point swing so one extreme state does not swamp the rest.

Presidential elections are compared with the previous presidential election. Midterms, counted by the House vote, are compared with the presidential election before them. The small dot beside the year shows the party of the president in office. Alaska and Hawaii sit in their usual insets.

What the waves show

Counting the states that moved each way at every step gives a plain picture:

  1. 2016Presidential vs 2012 12 Democratic39 Republican
  2. 2018Midterm vs 2016 48 Democratic3 Republican
  3. 2020Presidential vs 2016 44 Democratic7 Republican
  4. 2022Midterm vs 2020 8 Democratic43 Republican
  5. 2024Presidential vs 2020 0 Democratic51 Republican
  6. 2026Midterm vs 2024 · preliminary 49 Democratic1 Republican

Two things stand out. First, every midterm in the series moved against the party of the sitting president: toward Democrats in 2018 under a Republican president, toward Republicans in 2022 under a Democratic one, and toward Democrats again in the early 2026 count. Second, 2024 is the only step in which no state moved the other way: all 51 contests, the District of Columbia included, shifted toward the Republican candidate compared with 2020.

The nationwide two-party margin tells the same story in one line. It was Democratic +3.9 points in 2012, +2.2 in 2016 and +4.5 in 2020, then Republican +1.5 in 2024.

Read it with care

2026 is a snapshot. It uses live House results from NBC News, with between 74% and 99% of the vote counted per state, and Washington, DC has no House vote, so it shows no arrow in that step. Late-counted ballots can shift margins, and Alaska, the one state moving Republican, was the least counted.

House totals include uncontested seats. Where a party fielded no candidate in a district, its votes there are zero, which exaggerates the swing in some states. The Dakotas and parts of the Deep South show this most in 2022.

Midterm electorates differ. A midterm swing partly reflects who turned out, not only who changed their mind, and a House vote is not a presidential vote.
